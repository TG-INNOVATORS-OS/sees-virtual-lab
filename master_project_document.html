1. Executive Summary & Problem Statement

1.1 Executive Summary

Modern electric and internal-combustion vehicles expend significant portions of their onboard energy supplying auxiliary systems—including headlights, digital dashboards, HVAC fans, micro-controllers, and communication radios. Project S.E.E.S. (Self-Sustaining Energy System) is an applied research initiative designed to evaluate whether dissipated ambient energy (kinetic deceleration, exhaust/engine heat, and road-induced vibration) can be harvested and conditioned to supply auxiliary equipment.

Scientific Integrity Guarantee: S.E.E.S. is strictly governed by the First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics. It does not claim perpetual motion or infinite range. Every recovered watt originates from energy introduced into the vehicle and is bounded by conversion efficiencies and conditioning overhead.

1.2 The Problem Statement & Auxiliary Load Burden

In passenger vehicles and micro-mobility platforms, auxiliary electrical equipment typically consumes a continuous baseline of 200 W–1,500 W. In electric vehicles, this auxiliary draw directly depletes high-voltage traction batteries, reducing effective driving range by 10%–25% under night driving and active climate control.

The Core Research Question: Can a unified, multi-source recovery architecture capture sufficient ambient waste energy under realistic drive cycles to supply a measurable share (20% to 40%) of auxiliary demand without parasitic penalties on vehicle propulsion?

1.3 Project Core Objectives

  • Objective 1: Formulate rigorous mathematical SI models for kinetic, thermal, and vibration energy capture.
  • Objective 2: Design a Multi-Input Single-Output (MISO) power conditioning bus with passive Schottky isolation to prevent cross-feeding between mismatched sources.
  • Objective 3: Build an interactive computer simulation with thermodynamic assertion testing (Section 8 compliant).
  • Objective 4: Conduct benchtop experimental validation using accessible ATL lab equipment within a ₹0 budget constraint.

2. The Three Energy Recovery Pathways

2.1 Pathway 1: Kinetic Energy Recovery (Deceleration Capture)

The theoretical mechanical energy released during vehicle deceleration from speed $v_1$ to $v_2$ is governed by:

ΔEk = ½ m (v₁² − v₂²)    [Joules]

In S.E.E.S., this mechanical energy is converted to electricity using motor-generators operating in regenerative braking mode. The net electrical energy delivered to the auxiliary bus accounts for driveline, motor, and inverter efficiencies:

Ek,delivered = ΔEk · ηmech · ηgen · ηinverter    (Typical combined η ≈ 65% – 75%)

Kinetic recovery functions as a high-power intermittent surge harvester, yielding up to 750 mW on benchtop rigs and several kilowatts during vehicle deceleration.

In vehicles that already have regenerative braking, the kinetic channel only re-routes energy that braking would otherwise store in the traction battery; new energy comes mainly from the thermal and vibration channels.

2.2 Pathway 2: Thermoelectric Heat Recovery (Seebeck Effect)

Thermoelectric generators (TEGs) exploit the Seebeck effect: electron diffusion across a temperature gradient ($\Delta T = T_{hot} - T_{cold}$) creates an open-circuit voltage:

Voc = α · ΔT    [where α ≈ 0.032 V/K (calibrated value used in the model)]

The maximum theoretical conversion efficiency is constrained by the Carnot efficiency and the material figure of merit ($ZT$):

ηmax = (ΔT / Thot) · [ (√(1 + ZT) − 1) / (√(1 + ZT) + Tcold/Thot) ]

Commercial Bismuth Telluride (Bi2Te3) modules achieve 5% to 8% conversion efficiency at $\Delta T = 60\text{–}100^\circ\text{C}$. In automotive exhaust environments, TEGs provide a continuous baseload electrical supply.

Exhaust TEGs apply to petrol, diesel and hybrid vehicles. Pure EVs have no exhaust; they would use lower-temperature heat (motor, inverter, battery coolant), which gives much less power.

2.3 Pathway 3: Mechanical Vibration Harvesting (The Bimodal Principle)

Vibration recovery in S.E.E.S. is strictly distinguished between two distinct physical regimes:

Regime Transducer Type Power Output Range Intended System Role
Micro-Scale Piezoelectric Ceramic (PZT / PVDF Cantilever) 10 µW – 50 mW (Power density < 10 mW/cm³) Trickle charging, autonomous wireless IoT sensors, TPMS.
Macro-Scale Electromagnetic Regenerative Shock Absorber 20 W – 200 W per vehicle damper Direct auxiliary battery charging over rough terrain and speed bumps.
Scientific Clarification: Claims of hundreds of watts generated purely from surface piezoelectric patches are physically baseless due to high internal impedance (~kΩ to MΩ). S.E.E.S. correctly models piezoelectric outputs in the low-milliwatt domain while reserving macro-power for electromagnetic dampers. The bench model uses an optimistic 450 Ω effective source resistance, so real piezo output would be lower.

3.2 Power Conditioning & Combining Bus (Layer 3)

Connecting disparate generators with differing internal voltages and impedances poses a major engineering challenge. Direct parallel connection causes high-voltage sources (kinetic generators) to back-feed into lower-voltage sources (thermoelectric modules), destroying semiconductor junctions.

S.E.E.S. Solution: A Multi-Input Single-Output (MISO) diode-isolated architecture using ultra-low-forward-drop Schottky diodes (1N5819, $V_f \approx 0.22\text{V}$). This allows all three generators to feed a common DC bus independently without reverse conduction.

4. Mathematical Energy Balance & Simulation

4.1 Governing System Equations

Total Auxiliary Demand:     Eaux = Paux × t    [Watt-hours]
Gross Recovered Energy:     Egross = Ekinetic + Ethermal + Evibration
Net Useful Energy:     Enet = max(0, Egross − Eoverhead)
Auxiliary Demand Coverage:     Coverage (%) = (Enet / Eaux) × 100

4.2 Illustrative Benchmark Calculation (Section 7 Baseline)

The harvested inputs below (150, 100 and 20 Wh) are assumed for illustration; they are not measured. The bench produces under 1 W; vehicle-scale output is future work.

Consider an auxiliary vehicle load of 500 W operating continuously for 2.0 hours:

  • Total Demand: $E_{aux} = 500\text{ W} \times 2.0\text{ h} = \mathbf{1,000\text{ Wh}}\ (1.00\text{ kWh})$
  • Harvested Inputs: Kinetic: $150\text{ Wh}$ + Thermal: $100\text{ Wh}$ + Vibration: $20\text{ Wh} \implies E_{gross} = \mathbf{270\text{ Wh}}$
  • Overhead Losses: Power conditioning & switching losses = $\mathbf{30\text{ Wh}}$
  • Net Useful Contribution: $E_{net} = 270 - 30 = \mathbf{240\text{ Wh}}\ (0.24\text{ kWh})$
  • Auxiliary Coverage Ratio: $(240 / 1000) \times 100 = \mathbf{24.0\%}$
  • Primary Battery Reduction: Demand drawn from the main battery falls from $1,000\text{ Wh}$ to $\mathbf{760\text{ Wh}}$.

5. Simulated Bench Results (Virtual Lab) & Physical Test Plan

5.1 Calibrated Virtual Bench Implementation

To test the multi-source architecture in simulation before building physical hardware, an interactive, calibrated virtual benchtop was constructed in HTML5/JavaScript (sees_virtual_lab.html). The bench simulates real-time differential equation dynamics for a 12V DC flywheel motor, a TEC1-12706 Seebeck module, a 28 Hz cantilever piezo beam, a 1.0 Farad supercapacitor, and an auxiliary DC cooling fan and LED. All values in Table 5.2 are calculated by this calibrated model. They are not physical measurements; physical measurement is Phase 4 and is still to be done. The 1.0 F supercapacitor is rated 5.5 V; the model charges it only up to its 2.40 V 'Full' setpoint.

Diode-OR note: sources do not add. In Test 4 the kinetic channel (4.58 V) holds the bus, so the Seebeck (1.70 V) and piezo diodes are reverse-biased and stand by. When the flywheel slows below about 1,400 RPM, the Seebeck channel takes over the bus at about 1.70 V. This hand-over is how the surge and baseload channels complement each other.

5.3 Core Engineering Deductions

  • Kinetic as Surge Channel: Supplies peak charging power (749.3 mW) during deceleration events.
  • Thermal as Baseload Channel: Supplies uninterrupted background current (120.4 mW) unaffected by vehicle stopping or motion state.
  • Vibration as Sensor Supply: Generates 11.6 mW, confirming suitability for micro-telemetry rather than high auxiliary power.
  • Auxiliary Load Decoupling: The fan + LED load needs 108 mW (45 mA @ 2.4 V). While the kinetic channel holds the bus (flywheel above about 1,400 RPM) it delivers 749.3 mW gross (about 717 mW net), so the load runs at the full 2.4 V from harvested energy with no main-battery draw. The Seebeck channel alone gives 120.4 mW gross (about 113 mW net), which covers the load in power terms but only at 1.70 V (reduced fan speed); full 2.4 V operation needs the kinetic channel or an added boost stage.
